8 Change and adjacency

Transitions between dates

Knowing how much each class grew or shrank does not tell you where the area went. land_change() answers that question by comparing the maps pixel by pixel, at two or three dates, and measuring what stayed, what was converted and into what:

change <- land_change(maps, years = c(1985, 2024), language = "en")
change$plot
Class 1985 (ha) 2024 (ha) Gain (ha) Loss (ha) Net (ha)
Soybean 0 792 792 0 792
Pasture 717 53 12 676 -664
Mosaic of Uses 136 146 119 109 10
Forest Formation 200 107 20 113 -93
Wetland 147 100 3 49 -46

Between 1985 and 2024, 78% of the area changed class. Almost all of that is quantity change (820 ha), meaning that one class grew by taking the place of another; only 152 ha are swaps of position between classes. The forest illustrates the picture well: only 44% of the 1985 forest formation was still standing in 2024, and the 20 ha that appeared elsewhere did not make up for the 113 ha that were lost.

With years = c(1985, 2004, 2024), the diagram gains a third column and shows the trajectories across both intervals.

Adjacency between classes

land_to_graph() looks at neighbourhoods: it counts the contacts between pixels of different classes and builds the landscape network, in which each node is a class and the thickness of each link is the number of contacts between them. With weight = "boundary", the thickness becomes the boundary length in metres.

network <- land_to_graph(maps, year = 2024, language = "en")
network$plot

In 2024, the most connected class is wetland, which borders 8 classes along 29.6 km of edges. The tables network$node_metrics and network$edge_metrics detail degree, strength, centrality and the length of each boundary.
